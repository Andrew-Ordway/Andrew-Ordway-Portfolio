1   Client Pitch

Client Pitch

Team: Big Red Stompers   Clients: Cornell CALS Extension / E&J Gallo Winery / National Grape

Problem Statement

Spotted lanternflies (SLF) mainly target grapevines. As a result, grape farmers are trying to manage SLF populations in vineyards and nearby trees. SLF egg masses contain around 30-60 eggs, creating rapid population growth that affects later growing seasons. Current SLF population reduction methods, such as netting and manual egg removal are difficult to scale across larger vineyards.

Impact

Elimination of SLF eggs prevents the development of adult populations causing the most damage to vineyards. The average SLFs per vine starts low, increasing sharply following the transition from nymph to adult stages (Figure 1). As a result, attacking the root cause would protect regions like Lake Erie and the Finger Lakes from losses between $1.5 million in year one and $8.8 million by year three of infestation (Hayes).

Proposed Concept: Stomp n' Scrape

What it is: Handheld egg removal device.

How it would be used: Designed to crush SLF egg mass shells and immediately scrape them into a rubbing-alcohol-filled vessel to prevent hatching.

Why it's better than the status quo:

  • Reduces the chance of leaving viable eggs behind.
  • Minimizes mess and user discomfort by sealing egg material instantly.
  • Improved output and efficiency compared to manual scraping alone.

End-of-semester proof-of-concept: A functional prototype with a crushing feature, integrated scraper, and removable alcohol container, fabricated using 3D-printed components and accessible materials, and tested on simulated SLF egg masses to evaluate effectiveness and ease of cleaning.

Key Risks / Unknowns

  • 1 - Egg masses harden, making it difficult to ensure they are fully damaged. Two methods to kill the egg masses: crushing them and then scraping them into rubbing alcohol to kill the remaining ones.
  • 2 — Public aversion to kill/interact with SLF, as crushing egg masses may be off-putting. Test with user trials and gauge user comfort.
  • 3 — Many eggs are laid on high surfaces; 98% of eggs are found at unreachable heights (+10 feet) on trees (Krawczyk). Test attachments to extendable tools for users to reach higher egg masses.

Questions for Client

  1. What is preventing you from currently targeting egg masses? Is it location, time or resources?
    Decision affected: This would affect the aspects we want to emphasize in our design, like making it accessible to higher areas of the tree and ease to clean, based on client demand.
  2. Are there methods for reaching high areas that you are currently using?
    Decision affected: This would affect whether our product must account for reaching high-up areas, or if it can be used with existing tools.
  3. How much would you need to reduce SLF at the egg stage to be considered effective?
    Decision affected: This would affect how we determine the force needed and how we design our crushing/scraping mechanism.

Design Intent and Functionality

The prototype is intended to combine three main functions into one handheld device:

  1. Scraping: the scraper removes the spotted lanternfly egg mass off the surface.
  2. Crushing / Stomping: a spring-loaded internal stomper compresses the egg mass material to destroy the eggs.
  3. Collection: the lower opening funnels egg residue into an attached bottle for containment and later neutralization.

Parts List and Fabrication Details

The functional prototype consists of the following parts:

  • Housing – 3D printed at RPL; contains the sliding rail, supports the scraper assembly, and connects to the bottle interface.
  • Stomper Face – 3D printed at RPL; translates along the internal rail and compresses the egg mass during actuation.
  • Compression Spring – initially tested using McMaster spring 8969T983; additional spring options evaluated based on force and travel requirements.
  • Scraper Blade – thin metal or hard plastic blade seated in a channel on the housing; interfaces with the egg mass surface.
  • Bottle Interface – 3D printed funnel adapter connecting the housing exit to a standard rubbing alcohol bottle for containment.
  • Fasteners / Assembly Hardware – standard hardware (screws, pins) used to secure printed components during testing.

Purpose of the Prototype

The functional prototype was built to validate the core Stomp n' Scrape mechanism: the ability to simultaneously scrape an SLF egg mass from a surface, crush it with a spring-loaded stomper, and direct the material into a sealed containment vessel — all in a single handheld operation. The goal was to confirm that these three functions could be integrated without requiring fine motor precision from the user.

What Was Tested

  • Scraping effectiveness – the prototype was tested against simulated SLF egg mass surfaces to evaluate how cleanly the blade removed egg mass material.
  • Crushing force – the spring-loaded stomper was actuated repeatedly to assess whether the compression force was sufficient to rupture egg mass shells.
  • Containment – the funnel interface was tested to confirm that displaced egg material was directed into the bottle rather than escaping to the environment.
  • Usability – team members and test users operated the device to assess grip comfort, actuation effort, and ease of cleaning between uses.

Outcome

The prototype successfully demonstrated the integrated scrape-crush-collect workflow. The spring-loaded stomper provided sufficient force to compress simulated egg masses, and the funnel interface contained the displaced material effectively. Areas identified for improvement in future iterations include tightening the scraper blade seating to reduce flex during use, refining the spring selection for more consistent actuation force, and improving the seal at the bottle interface to prevent leakage during transport. Overall, the functional prototype confirmed the viability of the core concept and provided clear direction for the next design iteration.

BrahMos Ramjet Thermodynamic Analysis

With my newfound knowledge of thermodynamics from ENGRD 2210, I decided to apply what I learned to analyze ramjets and specifically the BrahMos Ramjet. On a high level, ramjet engines are open Brayton cycle engines used when aircraft are already traveling at incredibly fast supersonic speeds; however, ramjets have a couple key differences.

Firstly, instead of relying on a compressor as is used in conventional open Brayton cycles, the ramjet rather uses the aircraft's high speeds to compress incoming air. Because the ramjet does not have a compressor, it also does not have a turbine (which is typically needed to drive the compressor), allowing for all of the engine's work to be transferred into thrust. With no turbine, compressor, or shaft connecting the two, ramjets actually have no moving parts. Consequently, ramjets are only beneficial for aircraft traveling at speeds upwards of Mach 2 (meaning also that these aircraft must have some other booster to initially reach these supersonic speeds). In other words, ramjets are primarily used to "feed" off the aircraft's speed, becoming exponentially more powerful the faster the aircraft is moving.

Control Volume: Heat, Mass, and Work Transfers

So what exactly happens in a Brayton cycle with no compressor or turbine? First, the forward speed of the aircraft compresses air into the inlet. From there, the inlet directs the air into a diffuser, which slows it down—converting much of the air's kinetic energy to potential energy in the form of pressure and in turn heat. When a ramjet flies at supersonic speed, the incoming air must be slowed down before fuel can burn properly. This is done using a series of oblique shock waves that gradually reduce the air's speed, followed by a normal shock that brings the airflow to subsonic speed before combustion.

Once the pressure is great enough—typically when the air has slowed to Mach 0.3-0.5—the compressed air is mixed and ignited with fuel in a combustion heat exchanger. Through the combustor, energy is added to the airflow, and the resulting high-energy flow is accelerated through a converging-diverging nozzle to produce thrust.

A used change in design is that when the aircraft speed reaches above Mach 5, the ramjet engine begins to fail because the normal shock required to achieve subsonic speeds before combustion causes extremely high temperatures and low pressures.

Typical BrahMos Operating Conditions:
Cruise altitude: h = 10 km, M1 = 2.8
P1 = 26.5 kPa, T1 = 223 K
γ = 1.4, R = 287 J/(kg·K)
a₁ = γRT₁ = 299.3 m/s

1. Mass Balance

Step 1: Freestream velocity
V1 = M1 × a1
V1 = 2.8 × 299.3 = 838 m/s
Step 2: Freestream density
ρ1 = P1RT1
ρ1 = 26,500287 × 223 = 0.414 kg/m³
Step 3: Air mass flow
ṁair = ρ1 × A × V1
ṁair = 0.414 × 0.09 × 838 = 31.2 kg/s
Step 4: Fuel mass flow
ṁfuel = FAR × ṁair
ṁfuel = 0.057 × 31.2 = 1.78 kg/s
Step 5: Exhaust mass flow
ṁexhaust = ṁair + ṁfuel
ṁexhaust = 31.2 + 1.78 = 33.0 kg/s
Using the specific BrahMos data, I started by finding the mass flow rate of air entering the engine. Using the freestream velocity of the air (838 m/s, calculated from Mach 2.8 and the speed of sound 299.3 m/s) and the engine inlet area (0.09 m²), I determined that about 31.2 kg of air flows through the engine per second. The Fuel–Air Ratio (FAR) –which is the fuel to air mass flow ratio– of 0.057 is then used to calculate the fuel flow rate, giving 1.78 kg/s of fuel. By adding the air and fuel flows, I got a total exhaust mass flow of 33.0 kg/s, which is a number that will be used later to calculate thrust and energy.

2. Initial (Total) Conditions from Ram Compression

Step 1: Initial total temperature
T0T1 = 1 + γ-12 × M12
T0T1 = 1 + 0.2 × 7.84 = 2.568
T0 = 223 × 2.568 = 573 K
Step 2: Initial total pressure
P0P1 = (T0T1)γ/(γ-1)
P0P1 = (2.568)3.5 = 21.8
P0 = 26.5 × 21.8 = 578 kPa
Step 3: Shock losses
P02 = ηd × P0
P02 = 0.72 × 578 = 416 kPa
Step 4: Static conditions
T2 = T021 + 0.2M22
T2 = 6801.032 = 659 K
Step 5: Compression ratio
rp = P2P1 = 37226.5 = 14.0
Next, I calculated the total temperature and pressure of the air after it is compressed in the inlet. The freestream air that enters at 223 K and 26.5 kPa is compressed to 573 K and 578 kPa. After taking into account diffuser efficiency, the pressure drops to 416 kPa and the temperature rises to 659 K. This step illustrates how the engine converts the kinetic energy of high-speed air into pressure and heat, which are both crucial for the later combustion step.

3. Energy Balance (First Law of Thermodynamics)

Temperature-Entropy (T-s) Diagram

State Points:
● ① Freestream: T₁ = 223 K, P₁ = 26.5 kPa
● ② Diffuser Exit: T₂ = 680 K, P₂ = 360 kPa
● ③ Combustor Exit: T₃ = 2100 K, P₃ = 340 kPa
● ④ Nozzle Exit: T₄ = 1050 K, P₄ = 26.5 kPa
Processes:
① → ② Compression (Shocks + Ram)
② → ③ Combustion (P ≈ constant)
③ → ④ Expansion (Nozzle)
- - - Ideal Cycle
As described above, the ramjet T-s diagram shows a sharp rise in temperature and entropy during compression (① → ②), corresponding to the shocks slowing down the air and increasing its pressure. Next, during combustion (② → ③), the temperature increases further (at roughly constant pressure) as fuel is mixed in with and burned in the airflow. The temperature subsequently drops during expansion through the nozzle (③ → ④) as the air's thermal energy is converted into kinetic energy to generate thrust. Notice that unlike a closed-cycle engine, the diagram is not a loop because the ramjet is an open cycle, meaning that the inflow air exits at the exhaust without ever returning to its initial state.
Step 1: Fuel energy
Q̇fuel = ṁfuel × LHV
Q̇fuel = 1.78 × 42 × 10⁶ = 74.8 MW
Step 2: Inlet KE
KEin = ṁair × V122
KEin = 31.2 × (838)22 = 10.95 MW
Step 3: Inlet enthalpy
Hin = ṁair × cp × T1
Hin = 31.2 × 1005 × 223 = 6.99 MW
Step 4: Total input
Ein = Hin + KEin + Q̇fuel
Ein = 6.99 + 10.95 + 74.8 = 92.7 MW
Step 5: Exit enthalpy
Hout = ṁexhaust × cp × T4
Hout = 33.0 × 1150 × 1050 = 39.8 MW
Step 6: Exit KE
KEout = ṁexhaust × V422
KEout = 33.0 × (880)22 = 12.78 MW
Step 7: Energy losses
Elosses = Ein - Eout
Elosses = 92.7 - 52.6 = 40.1 MW (43.3%)
I then combined the energy contributions from fuel, air enthalpy, and the kinetic energy of the incoming air. Combining the fuel's 74.8 MW with the air's kinetic energy 10.95 MW and the air's enthalpy 6.99 MW, the total starting input energy is 92.7 MW. When leaving the nozzle, the energy output is 39.8 MW in enthalpy and 12.78 MW in kinetic energy, meaning that around 43% of the total energy is lost to irreversibilities like shocks and friction. From this percentage, I am able to calculate the fuel's efficiency in converting energy to thrust.

4. Thrust Calculation

Step 1: Momentum balance
F = ṁexhaust × V4 - ṁair × V1
Step 2: Thrust
F = 33.0 × 880 - 31.2 × 838
F = 29,040 - 26,146 = 2,894 N
F ≈ 2.9 kN
Step 3: Specific impulse
Isp = Fṁfuel × g0
Isp = 2,8941.78 × 9.81 = 166 seconds
The thrust was calculated by comparing the momentum of the exhaust (880 m/s) and the incoming air (838 m/s). This comparison yielded a net thrust of 2.9 kN and a specific impulse of 166 seconds. This step directly demonstrates not only the ability of the BrahMos ramjet to accelerate the missile but it also reflects the efficiency of its said thrust and propulsion.

5. Entropy Generation

Step 1: Sources
Oblique shocks: Δs1 ≈ 120 J/(kg·K)
Normal shock: Δs2 ≈ 180 J/(kg·K)
Combustion: Δs3 ≈ 650 J/(kg·K)
Friction/mixing: Δs4 ≈ 80 J/(kg·K)
Δstotal = 1,030 J/(kg·K)
Step 2: Rate of generation
Ṡgen = ṁair × Δstotal
Ṡgen = 31.2 × 1,030 = 32,136 W/K
Ṡgen ≈ 32.1 kW/K
Entropy increases were determined from the oblique shocks, the normal shock, combustion, and friction. The total entropy rise per kilogram of air is 1,030 J/kg·K, with a rate of 32.1 kW/K. The importance of this calculation is twofold. Firstly, this entropy increase reflects (and quantifies) the energy that the BrahMos ramjet loses to irreversibilities. Secondly, these calculations help break down precisely where and by how much the engine is less efficient due to shocks and mixing.

6. Efficiency Calculations

Step 1: Ideal efficiency
ηideal = 1 - 1rp(γ-1)/γ
ηideal = 1 - (14.0)-0.286 = 1 - 0.462
ηideal = 53.8%
Step 2: Thermal efficiency
ηthermal = Hout - Hinṁfuel × LHV
ηthermal = 39.8 - 6.9974.8 = 32.874.8
ηthermal = 43.9%
Step 3: Propulsive efficiency
ηprop = 21 + Vexit/V∞
ηprop = 21 + 880/838 = 22.050
ηprop = 97.6%
Step 4: Overall efficiency
ηoverall = ηthermal × ηprop
ηoverall = 0.439 × 0.976 = 0.428
ηoverall = 42.8%
To gauge the efficiency of the BrahMos ramjet, I looked at both the thermal efficiency and propulsive efficiency measures. The thermal efficiency (calculated to be 43.9%) indicates how well the engine converts fuel energy into mechanical energy. The propulsive efficiency (calculated to be 97.6%) reflects how effective the kinetic energy at the exhaust is to actually produce meaningful thrust. Combining these gives an overall efficiency of 42.8%, which represents the fraction of fuel energy that is ultimately converted into useful thrust.

7. Transition from Ramjet to Scramjet at Hypersonic Speeds

Given conditions at Mach 6.7:
Altitude: h = 30 km, T1 = 226.5 K, P1 = 1.197 kPa, M1 = 6.7
a₁ = γRT₁ = 1.4 × 287 × 226.5 = 302 m/s
V1 = M1 × a1 = 6.7 × 302 = 2,023 m/s
Ramjet (Fails)
Step 1: Stagnation conditions
T0/T1 = 1 + 0.2 × (6.7)² = 9.978
T0 = 226.5 × 9.978 = 2,260 K

P0/P1 = (9.978)3.5 = 992
P0 = 1.197 × 992 = 1,187 kPa
Step 2: Normal shock losses
For M = 6.7, normal shock relations give:
T02 = T0 (total temp conserved)

P02/P0 ≈ 0.0023 (severe loss!)
P02 = 1,187 × 0.0023 = 2.7 kPa
Ramjet loses ~99.8% of total pressure → combustion infeasible
Scramjet (Operates)
Step 1: Supersonic inlet
No normal shock → oblique shocks only
Step 2: Supersonic combustion
Combustion at M ≈ 2–3
Step 3: Pressure preservation
P02/P0 ≈ 0.3–0.5
Step 4: Useful thrust
Net positive thrust at hypersonic speeds
Scramjets avoid catastrophic shock losses by keeping flow supersonic
A key design change is that when the aircraft speed exceeds about Mach 6, the ramjet must be replaced by a scramjet. At these speeds, a ramjet becomes ineffective because it relies on a normal shock to slow the incoming flow to subsonic speeds before combustion. Starting at around Mach 6, this normal shock produces extremely high initial temperatures and severe total pressure losses, making efficient combustion impractical and net thrust minimal. Instead, a scramjet avoids these new shortcomings by using a series of oblique shocks (eliminating the normal shock entirely) and a long combustor which gives the airflow enough time to mix and burn with the fuel despite its supersonic speeds. As a result, the scramjet allows for supersonic combustion, which not only preserves pressure but also reduces both thermal and entropy losses, as evident in the calculations for a ramjet and scramjet both traveling Mach 6.7.

Arduino LED Matrix Pong Game

The Final Product

This project is a minimalist, hardware-based version of the classic Pong game, built using an Arduino UNO, a joystick module, and an 8x8 LED matrix display. The game runs entirely on Arduino, using no external screen or PC, and features interactive visual feedback for game events.

The player controls a paddle using an analog joystick to keep the ball in play. The game simulates basic physics, including bouncing off walls and paddle collision detection. When the player misses the ball, the game responds with a visual indicator (the entire screen lights up red) to signal game over before resetting.

Circuitry and Coding Components

Circuitry

The circuit uses a breadboard-based prototyping setup that organizes connections between the Arduino, joystick, and LED matrix:

Joystick Connections:

  • VCC (red) → Arduino 5V via breadboard power rail
  • GND (yellow) → Arduino GND via breadboard ground rail
  • VRx (purple) → Arduino A0 for X-axis paddle control
  • VRy (teal) → Arduino A1 (available for future features)

LED Matrix Connections:

  • Data (orange) → Arduino Digital Pin 6
  • VCC (red) → Arduino 5V
  • GND (white) → Arduino GND

The joystick module is mounted directly on the breadboard for easy access during gameplay, while color-coded jumper wires provide clean signal routing between components. USB power supplies sufficient current for the LED matrix at the reduced brightness level used in gameplay.

Software Architecture

The game is programmed in C++ using the Arduino framework and Adafruit NeoPixel library for LED control. Key software features include:

Game State Management:

  • Real-time position tracking for paddle and ball
  • Collision detection algorithms for walls and paddle
  • Game-over state handling with visual feedback

Input Processing:

  • Analog-to-digital conversion of joystick position
  • Threshold-based paddle movement with deadzone handling
  • Smooth, responsive control at 10Hz update rate

Display Rendering:

  • Efficient pixel addressing using serpentine matrix mapping
  • Frame-based animation at configurable update rates

Physics Engine:

  • Wall collision detection and reflection
  • Paddle hit detection with dynamic angle adjustment based on contact point
  • Ball direction varies depending on which part of the paddle makes contact
